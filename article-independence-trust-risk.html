Independence, Trust, Risk

September 2026 · Working with AI · Three filters for letting AI into real work

“Good enough” is one dial, and the decision to let AI into real work was never about one dial. Here are the three I actually use instead: how much rope, how much proof, how much it can break.

Everyone asks the same thing about AI: is it good enough to do my job yet? That’s the wrong question.

For a few months now, a system has been drafting essays under my name. It reads a map of what I care about, writes a full piece in my voice, and proposes it to me. Nothing reaches the site until I say yes. (This essay went through the same gate. It sat there for two months.) Running that taught me to stop asking whether the machine is capable. I ask three narrower questions now, in order: how much rope, how much proof, how much it can break. Independence, Trust, Risk.

Independence: how much rope.

The automotive engineers hit this problem first. The SAE’s six levels of driving automation, 0 through 5, aren’t really a measure of how clever the car is. They measure how much the human still has to watch. A Level 2 car steers and brakes on its own and still expects your hands near the wheel. A Level 4 car doesn’t.

Every task I hand a model gets a level. Some things it does and tells me about after: sorting my inbox, reformatting a messy table, pulling a rough first cut of a market scan. Lots of rope, because I’ll spot a bad job and the cost of one is a shrug. Other things it drafts and then waits: anything that goes out under my name, anything a client or a board will read. There the rope is short by design. The question was never “can it write the memo?” It usually can. The question is whether I want it acting or proposing. Those are different jobs, and confusing them is how people get surprised.

Trust: how much proof.

Trust isn’t a feeling. It’s a track record you’ve actually checked. I don’t trust the essay engine because the output reads well. Good prose is exactly where a model is most persuasive and least accountable. I trust it on the narrow thing I’ve watched it do correctly and verified, run after run: hold my structure, keep my analogies, flag its own banned words. On anything I haven’t watched it do repeatedly, trust is zero, and zero is the correct place to start.

This is where most people get it backwards. They extend trust because the first answer impressed them, then get burned on the fifth, when the model invents a citation or quietly drops a constraint. Competence in one demo is not reliability across a hundred runs. Earn the trust per task, on evidence, and pull it back the moment the model hallucinates something you’d have shipped without looking.

Risk: how much it can break.

The last filter overrides the other two. If this is wrong, what’s the blast radius, and can I undo it? A reversible mistake is cheap: a draft I can edit, a sort I can re-sort. An irreversible one is a different animal. An email already sent. A wrong number already sitting in front of a client. A post already public under my name. The more permanent the consequence, the less the model’s brilliance matters and the more that human gate earns its keep.

Notice the three can pull against each other. A task can be low-risk and still get short rope, because I haven’t built the trust yet. A task can be high-trust and still get a hard gate, because the downside is permanent and I don’t gamble with permanent. You hold all three at once. The trap is collapsing them into the single “is it smart enough” dial, because that dial quietly tells you to hand a confident, fluent, occasionally-wrong system the one job you can’t take back.

So I don’t ask whether AI can do the work. Of course it can do more of it every month, and that trend is the least interesting thing about it. I ask how much independence this task can stand, how much trust I’ve genuinely earned on it, and what it costs me if the thing is wrong. The capability belongs to the model. The judgment about where to spend it stays with me. That gate, for what it’s worth, is the same one the whole site runs on: I set out to build a website and built a system instead, and the human approval step was the part I refused to automate away.

The machines will keep clearing the “good enough” bar. They were always going to. The question worth holding onto is the other one: good enough to be trusted with what, exactly, and at what cost if you’re wrong?
